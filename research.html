01Family law · India

Unilateral Divorce and the Marriage Market: Evidence from India’s Triple Talaq Ban

With Rounak Bose

Abstract

Divorce reform can affect not only whether marriages dissolve, but also which existing marriages persist, who enters marriage, and the outcomes of marriages formed under the new law. We study India’s ban and September 2018 criminalization of triple talaq, an instantaneous form of unilateral divorce historically available to Muslim husbands. Using nationally representative surveys and retrospective marriage histories, we compare changes among Muslim women with those among non-Muslim women in a difference-in-differences framework. Among marriages formed before the reform, we estimate a decline in formal divorce, and the remaining married population shifts toward women who married younger, have husbands at least five years older, and report less say over their husbands’ earnings. These patterns are consistent with some marriages remaining intact that would otherwise have dissolved. First-marriage entry slows among women without primary completion, while the average estimate is imprecise. Our framework suggests that this decline could reflect greater reluctance among prospective husbands or fewer potential matches. Among marriages formed after the reform, divorce and emotional intimate partner violence decline without consistent socioeconomic upgrading. This pattern is consistent with a favorable behavioral response within marriage.

02Property rights · Pakistan

Intended and Unintended Consequences of a De Facto Inheritance Reform

With Sabrin Beg, Erica Field, Jeremy Lebow, Kate Vyborny, and Suzanna Khalifa

Abstract

De jure reforms to improve women’s legal rights are often not enforced in practice. In this study, we examine the effects of a land records reform in Punjab, Pakistan on de facto land rights of females, who are legally entitled to a share of parental land. This reform digitized and centralized land records and created a biometric verification requirement intended to limit females' exclusion from the inheritance process. Exploiting the staggered rollout of the reform across Punjab as well as the quasi-random timing of father/husband deaths, we find that it significantly increased the probability of female inheritance from 13% to 22%. However, we also find evidence of unintended consequences for younger women: they marry earlier and without consenting to their choice of spouse, marry lower quality spouses, are more likely to have children by their early twenties, and are more likely to drop out of school if they have large, expected increases in inheritance. These responses may be due to intrahousehold resource reallocation away from daughters in response to a forced inheritance transfer. The unintended marriage effects could also be driven by female land inheritance being used as a substitute for more liquid forms of dowry, as well as attempts to keep land in the family, either by marrying daughters off younger to exclude them from family negotiations or through consanguineous marriages.

03Gender & health · Pakistan

Post-Natal Son Preference and Inheritance Reforms

Abstract

How do households respond when administrative reforms make daughters' existing inheritance rights more enforceable in settings with strong son preference? This paper studies Punjab, Pakistan's staggered land-record digitization reform between 2010 and 2014, which digitized land records and introduced biometric verification of legal heirs during inheritance transfers. Linking district-level rollout to child-level data from the Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys and the Pakistan Social and Living Standard Measurement surveys, I estimate event-study effects on early-life mortality, breastfeeding, and vaccination. Among girls in landed households, infant mortality displays a clear upward post-reform dynamic: later cohort estimates are increases of 4.9 and 10.6 percentage points, although the survey-weighted average is an imprecise increase of 1.1 percentage points. Breastfeeding and vaccination estimates are also adverse but imprecise. Breastfeeding falls by 8.7 percentage points among girls in landed households, and vaccination falls by 7.4 percentage points among girls in rural households. I find no comparable patterns for boys or less inheritance-salient groups. The results suggest that making daughters' future inheritance claims more credible can worsen early-life outcomes for girls.

Policy writing

Education and accountability

Policy Recommendations for Advancing the Education Sector in Pakistan

Chapter 10 in Striving to Meet the Sustainable Development Goals: Next Steps for Policymakers and Practitioners, Asian Development Bank Institute · With Sabrin Beg

Abstract

We provide a description of accountability mechanisms in Pakistan’s education sector and compare the effectiveness of public and private schools. The analysis focuses on school inputs—such as amenities, teacher availability, and teacher quality—and school outputs, measured by student learning outcomes. The study highlights significant disparities between the two sectors. Despite public schools having greater resource availability, private schools often outperform them. We argue that accountability mechanisms play a central role in explaining these differences. In particular, effective incentives must reward performance in order to motivate school personnel to exert effort.
